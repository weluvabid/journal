LRU answers "who haven't we touched in a while?" Fine when sizes and rebuild costs are in the same ballpark. Bad when the cache holds a 40-byte report that took a serious computation and a fat blob that was cheap.

Cachewise's default score uses four things the caller can actually know: recency, a rough frequency, a recomputation weight, and a logical size. It will not predict the next request. It will stop pretending the last-access timestamp is the whole story.

```text
age     = max(0, now - lastAccessedAt)
recency = 0.5 ^ (age / recencyHalfLife)
reuse   = log2(1 + frequency)
cost    = log2(1 + computeCost)
score   = (recency × reuse × cost) / max(1, size)
```

Half-life is 60 seconds and it isn't a constructor option yet. Every half-life, recency halves. The logs keep a ridiculous `computeCost` or a saturated counter from scaling the score linearly forever. Dividing by size is the whole point: reuse per byte, not reuse in the abstract.

Neither input is measured. `computeCost` is a weight. `size` is whatever `estimateSize` returns. Inconsistent weights make a consistent mess. A bad estimator makes the denominator lie.

A `Map` of frequency counters grows with every key you've ever seen, even when the byte budget is holding. The sketch doesn't. Four rows, 8-bit counters, fixed width. Estimate is the minimum counter. Saturate at 255, halve on the half-life. Collisions overestimate; I don't keep a side table to "fix" that, because the side table is the unbounded structure I was avoiding. Object identity goes through a bounded LRU table. Two local symbols with the same description can collide. `maxTrackedKeys` sets the width of the sketch, not a key cap on the cache.

Picking a victim and admitting a candidate are different steps. Residents get ordered by ascending score until enough bytes fall out. The candidate is kept only if its score is positive and at least as high as every victim in that set. The plan is built first. Removals happen after the plan passes. Fail the admission check and the cache is untouched. Replacement works the same way: the old value is excluded from the budget math and disposed only if the new value gets in.

More than one victim means sort, then compare. Not a knapsack search. Single-victim is a scan. Multi-victim pays for the sort. That's the complexity I chose instead of maintaining a heap of scores on every read.

LRU stays in the same type, oldest access, admit once you've made room. Same budget, different question, so the bench can compare them without two caches.

The score does not know future traffic, real factory time, true heap size, or whether the caller’s weights mean anything. A phase change makes yesterday's hot key useless. That's why the next note is a benchmark with a narrow claim, not a victory lap.

| Entry | Recency | Frequency | Rebuild cost | Size |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Small report | 0.8 | 4 | 100 | 100 B |
| Large blob | 1.0 | 1 | 1 | 1,000 B |

Using the score above, the small report scores about `0.124`; the large blob about `0.001`. LRU prefers the more recently touched blob. Cost-aware scoring prefers the report per accounted byte. These are illustrative inputs, not a benchmark result.
